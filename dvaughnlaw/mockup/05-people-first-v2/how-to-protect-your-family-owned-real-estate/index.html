How to Protect Your Family-Owned Real Estate

PublishedOctober 6, 2026

CategoryWritten Blog Post

A family beach cottage, a mountain cabin, acreage that has been farmed for decades, or the home where three generations gathered for holidays: family real estate carries meaning that goes well beyond its market value. That is also why it so often becomes a source of conflict. When the plan is unclear, heirs are left to guess what you wanted, and siblings who once shared a summer house can end up sharing a courtroom.

Careful estate planning preserves both the property and the relationships around it. Here is where to start.

    1. Know How the Property Is Titled Everything begins with the deed. Is the property in your name alone? Held jointly with a spouse or child? Already in a trust or LLC? How title is held decides whether the property passes automatically, goes through probate, or creates questions for your heirs.Florida adds some factors to keep in mind: A title review is the right first step for any family property plan.
      • Homestead property. Florida’s Constitution limits how you can leave your homestead if you are survived by a spouse or minor child. A will that ignores these rules may not work the way you intended.
      • Out-of-state property. If you own a cabin in North Carolina or land in Georgia, your family may need a separate “ancillary” probate in that state unless the property is planned for properly.
      • Old or informal transfers. Deeds that were handwritten, never recorded, or that list someone who has passed away often cause title problems your heirs will have to fix.
    2. Define What You Want to Happen Before choosing legal tools, decide on the result you want. Common goals include: There is no wrong answer, but each goal calls for a different plan. Being clear about your intentions now is what keeps your family from fighting over them later.
      • Keeping the property in the family for future generations
      • Selling the property and dividing the proceeds equally
      • Leaving the property to one child and compensating the others with other assets
      • Letting a surviving spouse stay in the home for life before it passes to the children
    3. Choose the Right Legal Structure Depending on your goals, several tools are available: Each structure has trade-offs. For example, moving property into an LLC can mean losing the homestead exemption, a property tax reassessment, and documentary stamp tax. Good planning weighs these costs before any deed is signed.
      • Revocable living trust. A trust can avoid probate, name a trustee to manage the property, and spell out exactly how the property is to be used, shared, or sold. In Florida, a homestead held in a properly drafted revocable trust can generally keep its homestead tax exemption.
      • Enhanced life estate deed (“Lady Bird deed”). For many Florida homeowners, this deed is a simple and affordable way to pass property directly to beneficiaries at death without probate, while you keep full control during your lifetime.
      • Limited liability company (LLC). When several family members will share ownership, an LLC sets rules for decision-making, expense sharing, buyouts, and what happens if a member wants out. It can also add a layer of liability protection, particularly for rental property.
    4. Consider the Tax Impact How and when property passes can make a real difference in taxes. Property inherited at death generally gets a “stepped-up” tax basis, which can greatly reduce capital gains tax if your heirs later sell. Property given away during your lifetime usually keeps your original basis. Property tax reassessment and transfer taxes also come into play. Your plan should account for all of these.
    5. Plan for Ongoing Costs Real estate is never free to own. Property taxes, insurance (a big concern in coastal Florida), maintenance, repairs, and HOA dues continue long after you are gone. A strong plan answers ahead of time: Without clear answers, unpaid bills are one of the most common reasons family property ends up in a forced sale or a partition lawsuit.
      • Who pays these expenses, and in what shares?
      • Will a fund be set aside to cover them?
      • Can rental income offset carrying costs?
      • What happens if one heir cannot or will not pay their share?
    6. Talk to Your Family Surprises lead to conflict. Telling your loved ones what you intend, and why, sets expectations and gives everyone a chance to raise concerns while you can still address them. A family meeting now can prevent years of resentment and litigation later.

The Bottom Line

Planning for family real estate is about more than dividing property. It is about preserving a legacy, protecting your family’s finances, and keeping peace among the people you love. By addressing title, goals, legal structure, taxes, and ongoing costs now, you can make sure your family’s property stays in good hands for generations.
